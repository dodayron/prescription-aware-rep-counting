Adherence to home exercise is largely self-reported, and existing measures rarely capture the prescribed dose. Camera-based repetition counters could supply objective data, but they apply fixed, fitness-derived thresholds, whereas rehabilitation exercise is prescribed to a patient-specific range. We tested whether such counters misreport patients' exercise, and whether reporting attempts (adherence) separately from attempts at prescribed depth (fidelity) corrects this.
A two-state knee-angle rule using MediaPipe BlazePose (lite) on a Raspberry Pi 5 was extended with auto-calibration (thresholds from the user's first two repetitions) and a physiotherapist-set depth band (default 60–70° flexion). In this preliminary offline evaluation the rule was applied to KIMORE squat recordings (31 patients with stroke, Parkinson's disease or low back pain; 42 healthy adults; clinician scores; Kinect skeletons projected to a simulated side view) and to REHAB24-6 (9 healthy adults, 195 annotated squats, video with motion capture; same lite model). Intervals are subject-level bootstrap 95% CIs.
Fixed thresholds (160°/90°) logged zero repetitions for 58% [42, 74] of patients. These 18 patients made 50 attempts, of which 28 reached the prescribed depth. Across all patients the fixed counter logged 35 repetitions; the calibrated counter recorded 112 attempts, 87 at prescription (fidelity 78% [64, 89]; healthy 99%). Fidelity correlated with clinician score (ρ = 0.44 [0.19, 0.62]). Against motion capture in profile view, calibration raised recall from 54% to 97% [93, 100] with no false positives; knee-angle RMSE was 15.8°, falling to 4.5° [4.2, 4.9] with a linear correction learned on other participants. The Pi 5 ran the pipeline at 18–22 fps.
Fixed-threshold counting can report prescribed exercise as non-adherence. Counting attempts and reporting depth against the prescription gives clinicians adherence and fidelity from one single-board device. Validation on the device with patients, under ethics approval, is the next step.
